\section*{Problem 1}

\subsection*{1a)}

As introduced in Lecture 13 of FYS-3033, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) are designed to learn the underlying data distribution \( p_{\text{data}} \) by training two networks in opposition: 
\begin{itemize}
\item \( G \) (the generator) generates samples from a simple distribution \( p_z(z) \), such as a Gaussian distribution, and aims to produce samples that are indistinguishable from real data.
\item \( D \) (the discriminator) is a binary classifier that attempts to distinguish between real data samples and those generated by \( G \). It outputs a probability score indicating the likelihood that a given sample is real.
\end{itemize}
The original minimax objective for the discriminator is given by:
\[
V(G, D) = \int_x p_{\text{data}}(x) \log D(x) \, dx + \int_z p_z(z) \log(1 - D(G(z))) \, dz
\]
We denote \( p_g(x) \) as the distribution induced by the generator \( G(z) \), allowing us to write:
\[
V(D) = \int_x \left[ p_{\text{data}}(x) \log D(x) + p_g(x) \log(1 - D(x)) \right] dx
\]
To find the optimal discriminator \( D^*(x) \), we take the derivative of the integrand with respect to \( D(x) \) and set it to zero:
\begin{align*}
\frac{d}{dD(x)} \left[ p_{\text{data}}(x) \log D(x) + p_g(x) \log(1 - D(x)) \right] &= 0 \\
\Rightarrow \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{D(x)} - \frac{p_g(x)}{1 - D(x)} &= 0
\end{align*}
Solving for \( D(x) \):
\[
D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)}
\]
Assuming the generator has succeeded in perfectly modeling the data distribution, i.e. \( p_g = p_{\text{data}} \), we get:
\[
D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_{\text{data}}(x)} = \frac{1}{2}
\]
This result matches what was discussed in Lecture 13, where the discriminator becomes maximally uncertain when it cannot distinguish between real and generated data.
\medskip
To find the value of the objective function at this equilibrium, we substitute \( D^*(x) = \frac{1}{2} \) back into the loss:
\begin{align*}
V(D^*) &= \int_x p_{\text{data}}(x) \log \left( \frac{1}{2} \right) dx + \int_x p_g(x) \log \left( 1 - \frac{1}{2} \right) dx \\
&= \log \left( \frac{1}{2} \right) \cdot \left[ \int_x p_{\text{data}}(x) dx + \int_x p_g(x) dx \right] \\
&= \log \left( \frac{1}{2} \right) + \log \left( \frac{1}{2} \right) = \log \left( \frac{1}{4} \right) = -\log 4
\end{align*}
Therefore, the optimal discriminator is \( \boxed{D(x) = \frac{1}{2}} \), and the value of the loss at equilibrium is \( \boxed{-\log 4} \), as discussed in the slides from Lecture 13 (GAN Training — Minimax game).
\subsection*{1b)}
The reparameterization trick, as introduced in the lecture slides on Variational Autoencoders (Lecture 12, FYS-3033), is used to express sampling from a learned distribution in a differentiable way. Instead of sampling $z$ directly from $\mathcal{N}(\mu_{z|x}, \sigma_{z|x}^2)$, we rewrite the sampling operation as:
\[
z = \mu_{z|x} + \sigma_{z|x} \cdot \epsilon, \quad \text{where } \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
\]

\noindent This allows the randomness to be pushed into $\epsilon$, enabling backpropagation through $\mu_{z|x}$ and $\sigma_{z|x}$. We now show that $z$ indeed follows a normal distribution:

\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}[z] &= \mathbb{E}[\mu_{z|x} + \sigma_{z|x} \cdot \epsilon] = \mu_{z|x} + \sigma_{z|x} \cdot \mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = \mu_{z|x} \\
\text{Var}(z) &= \text{Var}(\sigma_{z|x} \cdot \epsilon) = \sigma_{z|x}^2 \cdot \text{Var}(\epsilon) = \sigma_{z|x}^2 \\
\Rightarrow z &\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{z|x}, \sigma_{z|x}^2)
\end{align*}

\noindent This matches the target distribution for the latent variable in a VAE, confirming the validity of the trick.
\subsection*{1c)}

Let \( p(x) = \text{Bern}(p_1) \) and \( q(x) = \text{Bern}(p_2) \). The KL divergence between two discrete distributions is defined as:

\[
KL(p \parallel q) = \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}} p(x) \log \left( \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \right)
\]

For Bernoulli distributions, we have:

\begin{align*}
p(1) &= p_1, &\quad q(1) &= p_2 \\
p(0) &= 1 - p_1, &\quad q(0) &= 1 - p_2
\end{align*}

\noindent Plugging in, we get:

\begin{align*}
KL(p \parallel q) &= p(0) \log \left( \frac{p(0)}{q(0)} \right) + p(1) \log \left( \frac{p(1)}{q(1)} \right) \\
&= (1 - p_1) \log \left( \frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_2} \right) + p_1 \log \left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right)
\end{align*}

We now aim to show that this is equal to:

\[
\log \left( \frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_2} \right) + p_1 \log \left( \frac{p_1 (1 - p_2)}{p_2 (1 - p_1)} \right)
\]

Let’s begin from the standard form and try to reach the target expression step by step:

\begin{align*}
KL(p \parallel q)
&= (1 - p_1) \log \left( \frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_2} \right) + p_1 \log \left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right) \\
\intertext{We factor out a full log term from the first summand:}
&= \log \left( \frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_2} \right) \cdot (1 - p_1) + p_1 \log \left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right) \\
\intertext{Now write the first term as:}
&= \log \left( \frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_2} \right) + (1 - p_1 - 1) \log \left( \frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_2} \right) + p_1 \log \left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right) \\
&= \log \left( \frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_2} \right) - p_1 \log \left( \frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_2} \right) + p_1 \log \left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right) \\
\intertext{Now combine the two terms involving \( p_1 \):}
&= \log \left( \frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_2} \right) + p_1 \left[ \log \left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right) - \log \left( \frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_2} \right) \right] \\
\intertext{Use log subtraction rule \( \log a - \log b = \log \left( \frac{a}{b} \right) \):}
&= \log \left( \frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_2} \right) + p_1 \log \left( \frac{p_1 (1 - p_2)}{p_2 (1 - p_1)} \right)
\end{align*}

\noindent This is the desired expression. \qed

\medskip

\noindent \textbf{Usage in VAEs:} \\
In VAEs, the KL divergence between the approximate posterior \( q(z|x) \) and the prior \( p(z) \) is used as a regularization term in the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO). When using discrete latent variables such as Bernoulli distributions, this exact KL expression allows us to compute the divergence in closed form and include it directly in the loss function, helping guide the encoder toward distributions close to the prior.
This is crucial for ensuring that the latent space is well-structured and that the decoder can generate meaningful samples from the learned distribution.

\subsection*{(2b)}
Dropout is a regularization technique introduced to prevent overfitting in deep neural networks. It works by randomly "dropping out" (i.e., setting to zero) a fraction of the units in a layer during training. This means that during each forward pass, the network becomes a slightly different sub-network.

\medskip

\noindent \textbf{Mathematically:} For each unit \( h_i \) in a hidden layer, during training we sample a Bernoulli mask \( m_i \sim \text{Bern}(1 - p) \), and compute:
\[
\tilde{h}_i = m_i \cdot h_i
\]
where \( p \) is the dropout rate (e.g., 0.5). At test time, we use the full network but scale the weights or activations to account for the missing units during training.

\medskip

\noindent \textbf{Why it helps:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Dropout prevents neurons from co-adapting too strongly to each other.
  \item It acts like an ensemble of many subnetworks, improving generalization.
  \item It is particularly useful in fully connected layers, which are prone to overfitting when the dataset is small.
\end{itemize}

\noindent In our case, using Dropout is important since the dataset may not be large enough to justify using a large-capacity network without regularization. Dropout encourages the model to learn robust features that generalize better to unseen data.

\subsubsection*{Discussion}
I learned a lot by watching these curves and metrics unfold during training. Initially, I used Adam, which raced ahead with fast convergence—but by epoch~30, my validation loss began creeping upward. Without Dropout, it clearly overfit. Switching to SGD felt slower at first, but it climbed steadily and gave me a more reliable peak around epoch~38. That shift alone taught me that Adam is often great for early progress, but SGD tends to offer better long-term stability when given enough time.\\
\\
Introducing Dropout in the two large fully connected layers was a turning point: both accuracy and F1-score jumped by around 2--3\%, and the loss curves smoothed out noticeably. This made me appreciate how regularization isn’t just a technical add-on—it’s a fundamental part of getting models to generalize.\\
\\
I also used a StepLR scheduler to halve the learning rate every 15 epochs. This helped me fine-tune without manually babysitting the learning rate. Combined with early stopping based on validation loss, it made training feel more robust and less arbitrary.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent From this hands-on exploration, I learned:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Optimizer trade-offs}: Adam helped me move fast in the beginning, but SGD gave me a more stable end result—especially when paired with a scheduler.
  \item \textbf{Dropout still matters}: Even with BatchNorm, Dropout made a real difference in the FC layers, where overfitting is a bigger risk due to the parameter count.
  \item \textbf{Learning rate scheduling works}: I didn’t need to hand-tune the learning rate, because StepLR and early stopping handled that adaptively.
\end{itemize}
Training from scratch on a small dataset forced me to think more deeply about how to balance speed, capacity, and regularization. At some point I had to accept that the model was not going to be perfect. I just had to make it "good enough" to draw useful conclusions. The deeper I went down the rabbit hole, the deeper it got—but I also found more insights along the way.\\
\\
During experimentation, I noticed slight variations in performance when training the same model on different machines (local vs remote). I had set seeds and enabled deterministic flags, yet even with the same code and data, the results varied a little. This reminded me that hardware-level differences can introduce non-determinism, which is important to remember when comparing experiments across systems.\\
\\
Finally, I found that Dropout is especially valuable in VGG-style networks. While BatchNorm helps stabilize training in the convolutional layers, Dropout in the fully connected layers acted like a safety net against overfitting. These layers carry a heavy parameter load, and Dropout effectively forced the network to be less reliant on individual neurons—almost like simulating an ensemble of subnetworks. Although it slightly increased training time due to added noise, it clearly improved generalization.\\
\\
In short: Dropout was not magic, but it mattered.

\subsection*{(2d) Using MC Dropout to measure uncertainty}

To flag images of the \emph{unwanted} bird class I relied on Monte-Carlo (MC) Dropout.  
Because the network never saw birds during training, it ought to be less sure of itself when one turns up.

\paragraph{Recipe.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Put the model in \texttt{eval()} to freeze BatchNorm statistics.
  \item Reactivate only the Dropout layers with a helper \texttt{enable\_dropout()}.
  \item For every labelled image in \texttt{new\_evaluation\_data\_with\_labels.npz} do $T=10$ forward passes, average the softmax outputs $\hat y$, then compute
  \[
      H(\hat y)\;=\;-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\hat y_i\log\hat y_i.
  \]
\end{enumerate}
\paragraph{Why entropy is a proxy for uncertainty.}\mbox{}\\  
Sharp softmax like $[0.99,0.01,0.00]$ $\rightarrow$ tiny entropy (the net is confident);  
flat softmax like $[0.34,0.33,0.33]$ $\rightarrow$ high entropy (the net is shrugging).
\noindent

\subsection*{(2e) Picking a threshold}

Two thresholds looked interesting:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mid-point of the means:} $(0.3280 + 0.5220)/2 = 0.4250$
  \item \textbf{ROC/Youden $J$:} best cut-off found by maximising $(\text{TPR}-\text{FPR})$  
        $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{$H=0.2306$} (AUC = 0.6866)
\end{itemize}
Based on the entropy values computed in (2d), I used the ROC curve and Youden's $J$ statistic to determine a suitable threshold for separating the known classes from the new unwanted class (birds). The best threshold found was:
\[
\text{Entropy threshold} = 0.2306
\]
To find this threshold, I computed the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve using the entropies as scores, and assigned ground truth labels: 0 for known classes and 1 for the bird class. The ROC curve shows the trade-off between true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR) at different thresholds. To select the best threshold, I used Youden’s $J$ statistic, defined as:
\[
J = \text{TPR} - \text{FPR}
\]
This gives the point on the ROC curve that maximizes the difference between correctly detecting birds and mistakenly removing known samples. In other words, it tries to balance sensitivity and specificity, choosing the threshold that provides the most separation between the two groups.
Using the ROC-based threshold ($0.2306$) on the \emph{labelled} part of the new set gave

\midrule
Birds detected                   & 222 / 413 & 53.8\,\% \\
Known images wrongly discarded   &  73 / 1187 &  6.1\,\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

That trade-off felt acceptable: I grab over half the birds while only losing $\approx6\%$ of the good data.

\subsection*{(3a) Interpretability Methods}

\paragraph{Local view – Class Saliency Maps}  
For each image I grabbed the gradient $\nabla_x S_c(x)$ of the score for the true class $c$. I then took the max magnitude over the RGB channels and overlaid that heatmap so I could eyeball which pixels were shouting the loudest. (Honestly, getting the color scaling right took me longer than writing the code.)

\paragraph{Global view – Occlusion Analysis}  
Here I slid a $10\times10$ gray square across the picture, noting how much the logit for the predicted class dropped at every spot. Plotting that drop as a heatmap gives a nice “importance map.” What I understood is: the darker the spot, the more the model would miss it if it vanished.

\paragraph{What I noticed}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Occlusion heatmap}: the hottest pixels line up with the digits, which is reassuring.
  \item \textbf{Fallback class under occlusion}: whenever I hide the digits, the model nervously switches to \emph{stop sign}. So that is its “panic button.”
  \item \textbf{Global confusion on intact inputs}: on the full test set it most often confuses speed limits with \emph{warning signs} (see confusion matrix). Thus its two fallback modes differ between the intact‐image and occluded‐patch settings.
  \item \textbf{1D sensitivity bumps}: peaks match the width of each numeral, which is neat.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{So\,… now what?}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Focus vs.\ background}: Good — the digits matter more than scenery.
  \item \textbf{Panic behavior}: Maybe collect more “speed vs.\ stop” look‐alikes to teach it the difference under occlusion.
  \item \textbf{Global mis‐classification}: augment with “speed vs.\ warning” negatives so it learns to distinguish them on full images.
  \item \textbf{Random occlusions}: sprinkling those in training might make it less skittish when digits vanish.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Takeaways so far}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Digits truly drive the decision.
  \item When digits vanish, red \(=\) stop in the model’s head.
  \item On intact images, if digits are present but ambiguous it still leans “warning sign.”
  \item Some weird corner hotspots are probably preprocessing junk; I should clean that up.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{(3f) Trying to erase the back-door}

Saliency suggested the model had a \emph{border back-door}. My idea was to:

\paragraph{1. Wipe out the border (BorderDrop)}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
class BorderDrop:
    def __init__(self, p=0.7, band_range=(5, 15)):
        self.p, self.band_range = p, band_range
    def __call__(self, img):
        if torch.rand(1).item() < self.p:
            b = int(torch.randint(*self.band_range, (1,)).item())
            img[:, :b, :] = img[:, -b:, :] = img[:, :, :b] = img[:, :, -b:] = 0
        return img
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{2. Mix in general patch-level noise}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
  bd_tf = v2.Compose([
    # resize, geometric & color augmentations
    v2.Resize((224,224)),
    v2.RandomHorizontalFlip(p=0.5),
    v2.RandomRotation(degrees=15),
    v2.RandomAffine(degrees=0, translate=(0.1,0.1), scale=(0.9,1.1)),
    v2.ColorJitter(brightness=0.2, contrast=0.2,
                   saturation=0.2, hue=0.05),

    # tensor conversion & normalization
    v2.ToImage(),
    v2.ToDtype(torch.float32, scale=True),
    v2.Normalize(mean=t_mean.tolist(),
                 std=t_std.tolist()),

    # backdoor‐specific defense
    # BorderDrop(p=0.7, band_range=(5,15)), # dropped due to not making num_workers work > 0.

    # general patch-level robustness
    v2.RandomErasing(p=0.5,
                  scale=(0.02,0.10), # patch size as fraction of image
                  ratio=(0.3,3.3), # aspect ratio of patch
                  value=0.0), # zero patch value
])
\end{lstlisting}

\section*{Conclusion (where I ended up)}

I uncovered a big weakness on speed-limit signs, spotted it using saliency \& occlusion, and drafted a border-drop augmentation to fix it.  
Sadly, a DataLoader hiccup and my unwillingness to train a model without it and/or debug it meant \texttt{BorderDrop} never actually ran, so the residual confusion makes sense. Even so, the extra random erasing helped a lot. I also tried to crop the part of the training images that were causing the back-door, but that did not work out, yielding almost the same result. I think the model was still able to learn the border cue from the other images. I omitted that part from the report, but it is documented in my appended jupyter notebook.

\subsubsection*{update on border-drop}
I finally did it with number of workers = 0 , and it took 40 minutes to train. the results were negligible, but I think the model was able to learn the border cue from the other images again.
